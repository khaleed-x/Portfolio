Imagine spending five years of your life in school. You survive sleepless exam nights, cram endless lecture PDFs, complete the compulsory industrial training, and defend your final-year project. You graduate, put on your NYSC khaki, and step into the Nigerian job market, only to be told at the gate: "Sorry, this role is for B.Sc. holders only."

If you are Nigerian, you don't have to imagine it. You have lived it, or you know someone who has.

For decades, the HND vs. B.Sc. dichotomy has quietly shaped careers, stalled promotions, and split graduates into two unequal classes. I'm a software engineer studying Computer Science inside the polytechnic system, and I see the irony up close every day.

How did two qualifications that demand almost the same academic grind end up so far apart in status? And is it finally changing? *(Status checked on 18 November 2026. This story is moving fast.)*

## 1. The origin story: two tracks, two jobs

Nigeria built its higher education on two tracks. **Universities** were meant to produce high-level professionals: the doctors, lawyers, engineers and teachers who would replace colonial administrators. **Polytechnics** were meant to train the middle layer: technologists and technicians who bridge the gap between top-level experts and manual workers.

The technical track started early. Yaba Technical Institute opened in October 1947. In 1977, the government created the National Board for Technical Education (NBTE) by law to build the middle-level technical manpower needed for the national development plans. In 1979, a decree re-established Yaba alongside six other new federal polytechnics.

The model drew heavily on the British technical-college tradition of that era. On paper, it was never a ranking. It was a **division of labour**.

## 2. What it was meant to solve

The goal was practical and genuinely noble. Nigeria did not want an economy full of graduates who could recite theory while foreign contractors were flown in to fix machines.

Polytechnics were designed to be hands-on, with the often-quoted split being roughly 70% practical work to 30% theory. You earn a National Diploma (ND) in two years, go out for a one-year Industrial Training (IT), then return for two more years to earn the Higher National Diploma (HND).

In theory, an HND graduate walks out with dirt under their fingernails, more industry-ready than a fresh university graduate.

## 3. Plot twist: theory without the degree

Somewhere along the way, two things turned that vision upside down.

**First, the "practical" part weakened.** Underfunding, outdated curricula and broken lab equipment make the hands-on promise hard to deliver in many polytechnics. In my own experience, a lot of the day-to-day classroom work looks much like a university's: reading handouts, cramming, writing theoretical reports. The workload is university-style, but there's no degree at the finish line.

**Second, the job market built a wall.**

- In the civil service, HND holders have long been capped at **Grade Level 14**, while degree holders can reach **Grade Level 17**, the top of the career ladder.
- HND holders were historically placed one level lower at entry: Grade Level 07 versus 08.
- To pass the ceiling, many have had to pay for extra qualifications such as a postgraduate diploma or a master's.
- Reporting on the Customs Service described degree holders being posted to superintendent roles while HND holders were put on guard duties.
- Many private employers followed the same pattern, filtering HND applications before skills could be tested.

Even lecturers feel it. In a university, an academic can rise to Professor. In a polytechnic, the academic ladder tops out at Chief Lecturer.

The biggest irony: the United Kingdom, whose technical-college tradition influenced this system, turned its polytechnics into universities in 1992. Nigeria kept the structure alive for decades after.

## 4. A long line of fixes that never fixed it

This isn't a new complaint, and the government has "solved" it more than once on paper.

- **2006:** the Obasanjo administration approved a policy to lift the ceiling on HND holders.
- **2007:** the Federal Executive Council under Yar'Adua directed that the ceiling be removed.
- **Around 2016:** the civil service resolved to move HND entry to Grade Level 08. A year later, Premium Times found many ministries and agencies still hadn't implemented it.
- **2018:** the education minister announced that polytechnics would award B.Tech degrees instead of HND. HND is still being awarded today.
- **2021:** the Senate passed a bill to abolish the dichotomy, and the House concurred in November. President Buhari left office in 2023 without signing it, despite appeals from ASUP and a 14-day ultimatum from polytechnic students.

Each time, the announcement was louder than the change.

## 5. 2026: the most movement in decades

This year looks different. Here is what happened, in order:

- **21 January:** Education Minister Tunji Alausa announced a plan to end the dichotomy by letting polytechnics award degrees.
- **May:** the National Universities Commission (NUC) told universities to stop HND-to-B.Sc. conversion and top-up programmes, reportedly over quality concerns and a worry that some had become money-making schemes. Many HND students felt sidelined, because the top-up was the route they used to escape employer bias.
- **15 October:** the Senate amended the Federal Polytechnics Act so that **federal polytechnics can award honours B.Tech degrees** in science, engineering and technology to ND, HND or postgraduate diploma holders, **subject to NUC accreditation**.
- **16 October:** President Tinubu signed bills upgrading the Federal Polytechnic, Kaduna, and Yaba College of Technology, where the technical track began in 1947, into Federal Universities of Technology and Vocational Studies.

**The catch:** the October amendment still needed harmonisation with the House and the President's assent when I last checked. Even once it's law, the practical impact depends on programme-by-programme accreditation and real implementation. And as recently as February, technologists were still asking for HND holders to be allowed past Grade Level 14.

## 6. The other side deserves a fair hearing

Not everyone sees the fix the same way.

- **"They're different programmes."** The NUC has argued that universities and polytechnics run distinct programmes, which makes simple conversion difficult.
- **"Fix the workplace, not the institution."** A Guardian editorial argued that equal treatment at work is a policy decision government can make, without turning every polytechnic into a degree-awarding one.
- **"Don't hollow out the polytechnic."** The polytechnic and university lecturers' unions (ASUP and ASUU) have objected to converting polytechnics into universities, warning that the future of this level of education is at risk.

It's a real debate. The question is whether we protect the polytechnic's purpose, or give its graduates the recognition they've been denied, and whether we can do both.

## 7. My take on this situation : one regulator, one name, many doors

I think the real problem is the referee, not the players.

Today, universities answer to the NUC and polytechnics answer to the NBTE. That means two bodies, two rulebooks, and two ideas of what quality looks like. When they disagree, students pay for it. A few years ago the NUC rejected the NBTE's plan to convert HNDs into degrees, and the NBTE publicly pushed back. Even this year's Senate amendment splits the job: the NUC accredits the new B.Tech degrees, while the NBTE keeps regulating the diploma tracks. And NCE programmes sit under yet another body, the NCCE.

So here is what I would do:

1. **One regulator.** A single body that oversees both the academic and the technical standards, so an institution doesn't need permission from two gatekeepers just to award a degree.
2. **A new name.** "Polytechnic" has carried decades of baggage in Nigeria. Rebrand it as a **University of Technology** or a **Technical University**.
3. **One system, many doors.** All higher education under the university umbrella. On the same campus, a National Diploma holder moves on to a B.Tech or an equivalent degree, an NCE holder moves on to a B.A., and regular students follow the 100 to 400/500 level route. Same campus, same standards, different on-ramps, and no more expensive top-up detours.

To be fair, this only works if the technical mission survives the merger. One regulator must protect practical training, labs and industry placement as fiercely as it protects academic standards. Otherwise we get what the unions fear: a hollowed-out polytechnic with a fancier name. And a new name can't fix an empty workshop.

But I believe one referee, one name and several doors is a cleaner answer than another round of announcements.

## 8. If you're an ND or HND student right now

- HND is still a valid, awarded qualification.
- The new B.Tech route isn't real in practice until the law is signed and programmes are accredited.
- Before you pay for any "upgrade" programme, confirm its NUC accreditation with the institution.

In the meantime, in fields like software engineering, many startups and remote employers care less about the letters on your certificate and more about what you can build and ship. Your GitHub and your projects speak louder than your certificate. That doesn't excuse the bias, but it's a path you control today.

## Summary

The HND/B.Sc. divide was never supposed to be a measure of intelligence. It started as a partnership between theory and execution.

But when underfunding stripped polytechnics of their labs, and employers punished graduates for holding a diploma instead of a degree, a well-meaning policy hardened into an academic caste system. Ending it isn't about changing four letters on a piece of paper. It's about making sure the laws signed this year change real pay scales, real promotions and real recognition for the work people actually put in.
